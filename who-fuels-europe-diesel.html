Who Fuels Europe?

Europe’s diesel margin is lower than America’s, yet a litre at a Belgian pump costs 60% more. America makes more diesel than it needs and ships the surplus across the Atlantic. Here is how the pieces fit: who makes it, who pays what, and who keeps the difference.

Static reference: 26 November 2026

Three things look contradictory. European refiners earn less on a barrel of diesel than American ones. The diesel itself costs almost the same on both sides. And the pump price is miles apart. They are all true at once, and they all come from one fact: America makes more diesel than it needs, and Europe needs more than it makes.

1. America makes more than it needs

Production

~5.2 mb/d

US refineries made about 5.16 million barrels a day of distillate over the four weeks to 13 November, from 16.8 million barrels a day of crude.

Exports

1.6 mb/d

The single largest diesel exporter in the world: US refiners shipped about 1.6 million barrels a day in August.

To Europe

~360 kb/d

On track for the third quarter, up from about 250,000 barrels a day before the war (S&P Global ship tracking).

2. Europe needs more than it makes

Diesel is Europe’s most-used fuel: trucks, farms, heating and a large share of its cars. Its refineries run about 12.2 million barrels a day of crude, but there is not enough refining capacity to cover diesel demand, so Europe imports. The EU and UK are bringing in about 800,000 barrels a day this month.

When Russia was cut off, Europe swapped a three-to-seven-day trip from the Baltic for long voyages from the Gulf, India and the US Gulf Coast. Every barrel now travels further, and Europe competes with the rest of the world for it.

3. Why the diesel costs about the same on both sides

Diesel is a global product. A European buyer has to pay roughly what it takes to pull a cargo away from its next-best buyer and across the ocean. So European and American diesel prices stay close, apart from shipping and the timing of cargoes.

On the same day and the same measure, the European benchmark margin is about $4 a barrel lower, roughly 2.4 euro cents a litre. That is the gap between the two refining benchmarks. It is not the gap between what refiners take home.

4. Why American refiners still earn more

Cheaper crude

~$10.70 a barrel

US refiners mostly run WTI and other American crudes; Europe runs Brent-linked crude. On 25 November Brent cost $94.69 and WTI $84.02. Same diesel price, crude $10 cheaper.

Cheaper energy

Gas and power

A refinery burns a lot of energy. American natural gas and electricity cost far less than European, a cost no crack spread shows.

Scale

Running flat out

US refiners ran near 96% of capacity in mid-November and pushed maintenance into 2027 to sell into a short world market.

The Brent–WTI spread is usually a few dollars. It is unusually wide now because Middle East risk hits seaborne crude like Brent harder than inland American crude. When that premium fades, the gap between European and American refiners narrows with it.

5. Why the pump is 60% dearer in Belgium

The diesel in the tank costs about the same. What differs is everything on top. In Belgium, excise, levies and the distribution margin come to about €0.95 a litre, and 21% VAT is charged on the whole price, another €0.38. In the US the federal diesel tax is 24.4 cents a gallon, about 6 cents a litre, plus state taxes.

The margin difference between the two refining industries is a few cents a litre. The tax difference is more than 80 cents. The pump gap is a tax story, not an oil story.

6. The weak link

Europe’s diesel now depends on three long supply lines, and each has broken or wobbled this year. Russian supply is gone. Middle East cargoes run past Hormuz. And in September, reports that Washington could impose a 90-day ban on diesel exports sent the EU to warn that such a ban would put both economies at risk. With a third of its outside imports coming from America, Europe has swapped one dependency for another.

That is why European diesel reacts so violently to news from places far away, and why the gasoil curve stays in backwardation even after the margin has halved: prompt barrels are scarce when the next ones are weeks away at sea.

Why it matters for inflation and gold

Europe imports its diesel in dollars. A weaker euro makes every cargo dearer, and a supply shock on any of the three routes reaches European pumps and freight costs quickly. That is part of why euro-area producer prices ran at 11% and the ECB has had to hike while its economy slows. For gold, European energy dependence is one more reason the rate cycle on this side of the Atlantic may outlast the market’s short picture. See Priced In, Not Over.

What to watch

  1. The Brent–WTI spread: wide means American refiners keep their edge; a narrowing spread means the Middle East premium is fading.
  2. US diesel exports and any talk of restrictions: a third of the EU’s outside supply rides on them.
  3. Russian export policy: the ban runs into January 2027; any easing adds barrels close to home.
  4. The European gasoil curve: backwardation easing is the sign the import chain has caught up. Track it on the Europe Diesel Pulse.
