Petrodollar to Chip-Dollar

Why the US dollar now depends on AI stocks instead of oil, why that makes it more fragile, and why the one big economy outside the new loop keeps buying gold.

For fifty years the dollar had a simple support beam: the world bought oil in dollars, and the oil producers parked their surplus in US Treasuries. A new Deutsche Bank note argues that beam is being replaced, and quickly, by semiconductors. The money still flows back to America. It just lands somewhere far more volatile.

The petrodollar, in one paragraph

Since the 1970s, the Gulf has sold its oil in dollars, earned large trade surpluses and recycled them into US government bonds. That gave the dollar three things at once: everyone needed it to buy energy, oil was priced in it, and the proceeds came back as steady, patient demand for Treasuries. Central banks and sovereign funds held the paper. It was a public-balance-sheet system, built on geopolitics, and it was calm.

The chip-dollar: same loop, new players

Deutsche Bank’s Mallika Sachdeva points to a milestone: over the four quarters to the second quarter of 2026, world trade in semiconductors exceeded trade in oil and gas. More important for currencies is where the surpluses now pile up. Chip surpluses in Korea, Taiwan, Japan and Southeast Asia have caught up with the Gulf’s petroleum surpluses.

Petrodollar loop

Gulf sells oil in dollars → surplus → US Treasuries. Held by central banks. Low volatility. Anchored in geopolitics.

Chip-dollar loop

Asia sells chips to US tech → surplus → US stocks. Held by private investors. High volatility. Anchored in tech leadership.

The difference is the destination. The Gulf bought bonds. Asia’s chip earners are buying shares, mostly in the same American technology companies that buy their chips.

Korea shows the whole loop

Korea is the cleanest case in the note:

  • Semiconductor exports are up more than 200% on a year ago, and the chip trade surplus is close to $250 billion.

  • Chips account for all of Korea’s overall trade surplus.

  • Over the past two years, Koreans bought five times more foreign shares than foreign bonds.

  • 70% of Korea’s overseas equity holdings sit in the US, and 90% for Korean households.

The US capital account tells the same story from the other side. Foreign purchases of US equities have passed $1 trillion over the past year, more than twice what foreigners put into Treasuries. Even foreign buying of US corporate bonds now outruns Treasury buying, and most of the jump in equity demand comes from Asia.

The dollar’s biggest new buyers are not central banks buying bonds. They are private investors buying AI stocks.

Why America stays at the centre

The factories are in Asia, so why do the dollars end up in New York? Because the profits do. Deutsche cites research showing about 70% of semiconductor industry profits go to US companies, and US firms control 87% of revenue in chip design. The manufacturing countries are US allies. The value chain starts and ends with American companies.

The runway is long. Hyperscalers have committed close to $8 trillion of capital spending through 2031. Chips make up more than half the cost of an AI data centre, which implies roughly $4 trillion of further chip demand from AI alone. As long as that spending holds, the chip-dollar loop keeps feeding the dollar.

Where the chip-dollar can break

1. It feeds itself

Asian surpluses buy US AI stocks. Those surpluses only exist because US AI companies buy chips. If demand for computing power falls behind the investment, earnings forecasts drop, chip orders drop and the foreign bid for US stocks dries up at the same time.

2. Stocks are a shaky anchor

Realised volatility in the S&P 500 has been about three times that of 10-year Treasuries. The Nasdaq swings about twice as much as the S&P, and the chip index more again. A dollar that leans on equity inflows inherits that volatility.

China is not in the loop, and it buys gold

China is the outlier. It runs a manufactured-goods surplus of more than $2 trillion, yet a persistent deficit in semiconductors, and it does not recycle its earnings into US markets. Less than 20% of China’s net international assets are portfolio investments, against nearly 50% for Korea, Japan and Taiwan. Its preference is gold and hard assets.

That matters for anyone reading the gold market. The largest surplus economy in the world is outside the chip-dollar loop by choice, and its spare savings go into metal. You can follow how hard Chinese buyers are paying up for that metal every trading morning in the Shanghai and India gold premiums.

What this means for gold

Deutsche’s conclusion is that dollar dominance now rests on US technology leadership instead of geopolitical leadership, and on private balance sheets instead of public ones. It helps explain why the dollar has held up even as foreign official demand for Treasuries faded: Asian chip money stepped in through the stock market.

My read, as a trader rather than an economist:

  • While AI spending rises, the chip-dollar is a support for the dollar, and a strong dollar is a headwind for gold priced in dollars.

  • When the AI trade cracks, the same flow that props up US stocks is the flow that props up the currency. Both can leave together, and the dollar move could be sharper than in past equity sell-offs. That is the kind of fragility gold has historically hedged.

  • The bond side still matters. If the Fed hikes into a wobbling AI trade while long yields stay high, the pressure lands on the real yield first. The real rates page is where that shows up.

What to watch

  1. Nvidia and the chip index. Nvidia reports on 17 November. A miss that hits the chip index hard is the first test of whether the dollar now trades with AI stocks.

  2. Korea and Taiwan. The Kospi, the won and the Taiwan dollar are the front line of the chip-dollar. Weakness there before Wall Street is an early warning.

  3. The dollar on chip sell-off days. If the DXY starts falling on days when chip stocks fall, the link Deutsche describes is live. The Dollar Balance page tracks the dollar side.

  4. The Shanghai premium. China’s appetite for metal is the counterweight. A rising premium while US tech wobbles says the hard-asset camp is buying.
