The Taiwan Stress Test

What happens to your money if the Taiwan Strait closes? Three scenarios, six channels and a simulator you can run on your own portfolio, built from everything this week taught us.

Written 17 November 2026, the day before the Trump–Xi talks in Shenzhen · a stress test, not a forecast

This week gave us four cracks: a chipmaker under a cloud, systems failing one after another, a gold short that never covers and a bond market offering a cushion. Then a reader asked the obvious next question: what if the shock comes from the Taiwan Strait? So we ran the test.

▶ Watch the video version · 2:01

A word on what this is. Nobody can forecast a war, and most analysts see a blockade or “quarantine” as far more likely than an invasion. A stress test does something more useful: it asks what breaks first, in what order, and what holds. Every number below is an assumption, written down so you can disagree with it.

Where we stand today

On 12 November, Xi Jinping renewed his call for Taiwan’s reunification, on the same day Taiwan counted 29 Chinese military aircraft around the island. Tomorrow and Thursday, Trump and Xi are expected to talk on the sidelines of the APEC summit in Shenzhen. Neither Beijing nor Taipei reports an imminent military confrontation, and China’s military command is still recovering from a purge of senior generals in late 2025 and early 2026, which cuts both ways for readiness.

The tension is real but not new. What is new is how little cushion the markets have left, and that is what this week was about.

The three scenarios

A
Grey-zone squeeze, 2–4 weeks. Large drills, coast-guard “inspections”, cyberattacks, ships rerouted. Chips keep flowing, but insurance and shipping costs jump and markets price the risk.
B
Quarantine or blockade, 1–3 months. Shipping in and out of Taiwan stops. Chip deliveries halt, the AI build-out freezes, energy shipments to Japan and Korea reroute.
C
Long blockade and Western sanctions, 6 months or more. Everything in B, plus sanctions on China in the style of Russia in 2022, frozen reserves and a split in global trade.

How the shock travels

Each step below is something we wrote about this week or earlier. That is the point of the test: the shock does not invent new problems. It hits the ones already sitting there.

1. Chips: the epicentre

About 90% of the world’s most advanced chips come from Taiwan. Nvidia designs them; TSMC makes them. In scenario B there is no “revenue air gap” of the kind Culper Research argued in Four Cracks, One Question. There is no product. And the rare earths and critical minerals behind every chip are already a pressure point, as we showed in Rare Earths.

2. The chip-dollar breaks both ways

In Petrodollar to Chip-Dollar we followed Deutsche Bank’s argument: Asia’s chip surpluses, Taiwan’s and Korea’s above all, now recycle into US AI stocks, more than $1 trillion of foreign buying in a year. A Taiwan shock cuts both ends of that loop at once: the chips stop and the money stops. AI stocks fall, and the foreign bid that held them up goes with them.

That is why we expect the dollar to behave oddly. In the first days, the reflex is to buy dollars. But the dollar’s newest support beam is the very thing breaking, so the bounce may fade faster than in past crises. The Dollar Balance page is where that tug-of-war will show.

3. AI debt becomes a bank problem

The banks are already trying to get out. HSBC has reportedly sold its $300 million piece of the Stargate New Mexico loan at about 85 cents on the dollar, and ING has put about $500 million of data-centre loans up for auction, with bids due on Thursday. Data centres that cannot get new GPUs cannot earn the revenue to service those loans. PIMCO named “AI overinvestment” as a key risk; in scenario B it becomes a credit event, and it travels through the same kind of plumbing that failed last week, when one bank’s bug delayed payments at six others.

4. Oil and diesel: a market with no cushion

The glut headlines describe oil on paper and at sea. Onshore stocks are still falling, and the IEA calls diesel tightness the “overriding concern”, with European diesel about 77% above pre-war levels. We showed how the bottleneck moved from crude to refineries in The Strait Is Closed. The Oil Is Flowing. and in Thirty-Year Yields at a 24-Year High. Add rerouted shipping around Taiwan, war-risk insurance and Japan and Korea scrambling for fuel, and diesel moves first. Watch the crack spreads and the Hormuz tracker: two straits under pressure at once is the nightmare case.

5. Bonds: PIMCO’s cushion meets inflation

PIMCO’s case is a real yield of about 3%: a bond index in the mid-5% range against core inflation in the mid-2% range. An energy shock plus a supply shock is stagflation, the quadrant we mapped in The Protection Map. Treasuries catch a safety bid at first, then inflation eats the cushion. Defence spending deepens the “US fiscal sustainability” risk PIMCO itself names, the loop we described in The Sovereign Debt Loop. With the Fed already priced to hike, December Decides would suddenly look very different.

6. Gold: the dip, then the trust trade

Here the honest answer has two parts.

  • The first days can hurt. In a panic, investors sell what they can to meet margin calls, and gold is liquid. In March 2020 gold fell about 12% in under two weeks before going on to a record by August. We saw the same reflex on a smaller scale in Silver’s Bad Monday. A drop on day one is not the verdict.

  • Then trust takes over. In scenario C, Western sanctions on China would make every central bank with dollar reserves ask whether theirs could be frozen too. That question, after Russia in 2022, is what turned central banks into steady gold buyers. It is also the thread running through When Rates Rise and Confidence Doesn’t, The Third Confidence and BRICS and global finance.

  • The East pays up. When Asia wants metal, it shows in the Shanghai premium first, as it did in January’s blow-off top, described in Gold Has Three Prices, and in Price Down, Metal Up. Chinese banks have already been taking the leverage out but not the gold.

  • The paper short meets the physical buyer. On COMEX, the swap dealers, including the bullion banks, are net short about 640 tonnes, half of all open interest, barely changed since January. A world rushing for physical metal is when “what if they have to cover?” stops being theoretical.

The simulation

We gave each scenario a set of assumed moves for two horizons: the first week, when panic and liquidation dominate, and three months, when the economic damage has sunk in. They are built from two analogues, March 2020 and February–March 2022 (when gold rose about 9% in two weeks and Brent briefly touched about $139 from under $100), adjusted for this week’s starting point: little oil cushion, AI debt already for sale and a concentrated paper short in gold.

Early warning: six lights to watch

A Taiwan shock would not arrive out of nowhere. These are the markets that move before the headlines, most of which you can already follow on the site and the desk.

  1. The Taiwan dollar and the Kospi. The front line of the chip-dollar. Weakness there before Wall Street is the first tell.

  2. The Shanghai gold premium. China paying up for metal. Updated every trading morning on the Asia premiums page.

  3. Diesel crack spreads. The oil market’s missing cushion, on the crack spreads page.

  4. The ING loan auction and AI credit. Bids are due on Thursday 19 November. The price tells you what AI debt is worth before any shock.

  5. The COMEX swap-dealer short. The next Commitments of Traders report comes on Friday. A sudden drop means someone is covering.

  6. War-risk insurance in the Strait. Shipping prices risk before markets do, as it did around Hormuz.

What this means for you

The test does not say “sell everything and buy gold”. It says something narrower and more useful. In every scenario, the assets that depend on someone else keeping a promise, a chipmaker’s supply chain, a bank’s systems, a bond’s real yield, a paper contract’s delivery, take the hit first. The one asset that is not a claim on anyone takes the first-week dip and then does its job.

Two practical points from earlier work matter here. First, gold is the core, not the house: the Protection Map shows what else protects you in stagflation. Second, where your gold sits decides how safe it really is, as we argued in Could Europe Take Your Gold? A shock that brings sanctions and capital controls is exactly when storage location and dealer quality matter, which is why we compared them in Sixteen Dealers, Six Questions and looked at old coins in Junk Silver in Europe.

You don’t own gold for the crisis you can predict. You own it for the one you can’t.
