Rare Earths

The 17 metals inside every electric motor, wind turbine, smartphone and guided missile. What they are, what each one does, where they come from, why one country controls them, and how an investor gets exposure.

Written 7 November 2026, three days before a Chinese export-control deadline

3days to 10 Nov
The deadline this week

On 10 November 2026 China's one-year suspension of its October 2025 export controls expires in law. Both Washington and Beijing said in late September that the trade truce runs to 10 January 2027, but by the end of October Beijing had not published the notice to change it. Separately, China's April 2025 licences on seven medium and heavy rare earths were never suspended and still apply.

What they are

Rare earths are 17 metallic elements: the 15 lanthanides in the periodic table plus scandium and yttrium. They are not rare in the Earth's crust. What is rare is finding them concentrated enough to mine, and separating them is the hard part: they are chemically so alike that a refinery needs hundreds of solvent-extraction stages to pull one from another.

Light rare earths

The volume

Lanthanum, cerium, praseodymium and neodymium make up most of what is mined. Neodymium and praseodymium (sold together as NdPr) are where most of the value is, because they make the strongest permanent magnets.

Heavy rare earths

The scarcity

Dysprosium, terbium and the other heavies are found in small amounts, mostly in clay deposits in southern China and Myanmar. A few percent of dysprosium or terbium lets a magnet keep its strength in a hot electric motor.

The magnet

Why it matters

Magnets are the largest end use of rare earths worldwide. A neodymium-iron-boron magnet powers the motor of an electric car, the generator of a wind turbine, a hard drive, a speaker and the fins of a missile.

Where they come from

Mining is only half the story. China mined about 70% of the world's rare earths in 2025, but it refines about 90% of them, and nearly all of the heaviest. Ore from the US, Myanmar and elsewhere has long been shipped to China to be separated.

China

Bayan Obo and the southern clays

Bayan Obo in Inner Mongolia is the world's largest rare-earth mine, mostly light rare earths. The heavies come from ion-adsorption clays in southern China and, increasingly, from Myanmar's Kachin State.

United States · Australia

Mountain Pass and Mount Weld

MP Materials' Mountain Pass mine in California is the largest outside China. Lynas mines Mount Weld in Western Australia and separates the ore in Malaysia, the largest producer of separated rare earths outside China.

The next wave

Brazil, Greenland, Vietnam

Brazil holds the second-largest reserves; its Serra Verde clay mine produces heavy rare earths with US-linked price floors. Greenland and Vietnam hold large deposits that are not yet in production at scale.

The control: how China uses it

In April 2025 China required export licences for seven medium and heavy rare earths (samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium and yttrium), their metals, oxides and the magnets that contain them. Car makers in Europe and the US reported production stops within weeks. In October 2025 it added five more elements and rules that could reach foreign products containing as little as 0.1% Chinese-origin rare earths by value. After the Trump–Xi meeting in November 2025 China suspended the October rules for one year; the April licences stayed.

China does not need to stop exports to move the market. It only needs to make every shipment depend on a licence. The licence is the lever, and it is still in place.

This is the second time. In 2010, during a territorial dispute with Japan, China curbed rare-earth exports; prices multiplied within months, then collapsed as buyers cut their use and new mines outside China opened. Investors who bought the spike lost most of it.

Two prices for the same metal

There is no liquid exchange-traded futures market for rare earths outside China. Prices are assessments by price reporters, and since the licences they come in two versions: inside China, and outside it.

Rare Earth Exchanges assessments, mid-October 2026. In China, NdPr oxide traded at 710,000–714,000 yuan per tonne in the week to 5 November (Shanghai Metals Market), after dipping below 700,000. The gap is the price of a licence: heavy rare earths that cannot leave China freely are worth ten times more outside it.

Western governments are now writing price floors into contracts. The US Department of Defense took a $400 million stake in MP Materials in July 2025 and guaranteed $110 per kg for its NdPr for ten years. A floor turns a volatile commodity into something closer to a regulated utility, for the companies that have one.

How to get exposure

You cannot buy rare earths the way you buy a gold bar or a gold ETC. Exposure comes through the companies that mine, separate and turn them into magnets, or through a fund that holds those companies. That makes it an equity and policy bet, not a store of value.

Fund · the simplest route

VanEck Rare Earth and Strategic Metals REMX

Holds the producers worldwide. Listed in New York (REMX) and in Europe as a UCITS ETF (ISIN IE0002PG6CA6, ticker REMX on Euronext, Xetra and Milan). It also holds lithium and other strategic-metal companies, so it is broader than rare earths alone.

Canada · TSX: NEO

Neo Performance Materials

Makes rare-earth materials and magnets, with a separation plant and a magnet factory in Estonia: one of the few routes to a European magnet supply chain.

China's own champions, such as China Northern Rare Earth (Shanghai: 600111), are the low-cost producers but are hard for most European investors to buy directly. Smaller developers and explorers carry far more risk: many have no revenue, and their value depends on a price that a single government decision can move.

How this fits the framework: gold is insurance you hold for years. Rare earths are a strategic theme you size small. A broad fund spreads the company risk; producers with a government price floor carry less price risk than those without; and every position should survive the day China decides to sell more, not less.

What to watch

  1. 10 November 2026: whether Beijing publishes a notice extending the suspension of its October 2025 rules, lets it lapse, or reinstates part of it.
  2. 10 January 2027: the end of the US–China truce as announced. The next bargaining point.
  3. The licence gap: the price of dysprosium and terbium outside China against inside it. A narrowing gap means licences are flowing; a widening one means they are not.
  4. China's monthly export data: rare-earth and magnet export volumes show whether licences are being granted.
  5. Western heavy rare earths: MP Materials' first dysprosium and terbium from Mountain Pass, expected this year, and Lynas' heavy output.
