The Looming Bill

Euroclear, the yen and Europe’s exit door. The bill for a debt-heavy world is being passed around, and the clues show up in the money flows long before the headlines.

Every major economy is carrying more debt at higher interest rates than it can comfortably pay. Someone will pay the bill. Three recent events show how quietly it is being passed around, and why Europe, and Belgium in particular, sits closer to the exit door than most savers think.

To save the yen, Washington spent euros

On 31 July and 1 August 2026, the US Treasury bought yen together with Japan, the first joint intervention in more than a decade. The detail that matters: it paid for the yen by selling euros, not dollars. According to a handwritten note from Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent that surfaced in reporting, the operation was worth $5 to 10 billion, executed by the New York Fed.

Funding it in euros meant the yen could be lifted without pushing the dollar down. The yen rose more than 1% that day, then gave back about half of the gain within weeks; Deutsche Bank’s George Saravelos called the move ineffective. Whatever its success, the choice of currency is the signal: when the US defended its ally’s currency, the cost was booked to Europe’s.

To protect the yen, Washington sold euros. When a support operation needs a currency to lean on, it leans on someone else’s.

Japan is coming home, from America first

Japan’s 10-year yield reached a 30-year high of 3.122% in October. For the first time in decades, Japanese savers earn something at home, and money that spent years abroad is starting to return. Japanese investors sold about ¥5.08 trillion more foreign bonds than they bought in the first nine months of the year, the most since 2022.

A common assumption is that Japan is dumping Europe. The Bank of Japan’s regional data for January to August says otherwise: net sales of about ¥4.74 trillion of US bonds, against a small net purchase of about ¥356 billion in Europe. Within Europe it was mixed: Italian bonds bought, French and German bonds sold. Commerzbank reaches the same conclusion: Japanese selling has hit US bonds most, supporting the euro, and Japanese investors have returned as buyers of French bonds in the last three months.

One caution: these flows are published with a delay and record where bonds are held, not always who owns them. The picture can change with the next release.

Euroclear: €185 billion in Brussels

Since 2022, Russia’s central bank reserves held in Europe have been frozen, about €210 billion in total, roughly €185 billion of it at Euroclear, the securities depository in Brussels. In December 2025, the European Commission proposed lending that money to Ukraine as a “reparations loan”. Belgium, led by Prime Minister Bart De Wever, blocked it, fearing Russian retaliation and liability landing on Belgium.

EU leaders chose joint borrowing instead: a €90 billion interest-free loan to Ukraine, funded by 24 of the 27 member states. The Russian assets stay frozen indefinitely, and the EU reserved the right to use them later to repay the loan. Fitch had warned that a reparations loan could open a gap in Euroclear’s balance sheet.

Moscow has answered in court. Russia’s central bank sued Euroclear for about 18.2 trillion roubles (around $229 billion). A Moscow court ruled against Euroclear in May 2026 and an appeals court upheld the ruling in July. Euroclear rejects it and keeps the assets frozen. The central bank says it will decide later how to enforce the ruling, including against Euroclear assets outside Russia.

Why this weighs on euro bonds

No rating agency has downgraded a eurozone country over Euroclear. The cost is quieter, and it works through trust. A reserve currency is only as good as the promise that the holder’s money stays theirs. Once one central bank’s reserves can be frozen indefinitely, and possibly used, every central bank outside the West has to ask whether its own euro holdings could be next.

The cleanest answer to that question is gold in a vault at home: no issuer, no depository, nothing to freeze. Central banks have been buying gold at a pace not seen in over a decade since the 2022 freeze (we looked at that shift in Gold Has Overtaken US Treasuries). The cost to Europe is not one dramatic downgrade but a slow loss of demand for euro debt, at exactly the moment Europe needs to borrow more.

Bonds first, streets last

The countries that can least afford that are already visible in the bond market. On 13 October, Belgium borrowed for ten years at about 4.33%, level with Greece at 4.36%. France paid 4.84%, more than Italy. Over twelve months, France’s spread over the German Bund widened by about 59 basis points and Belgium’s by 33, the two worst in the eurozone (details in Belgium: France in the Shadows).

Then the bill reached the streets. On 9 October, tens of thousands marched in Brussels against about €10 billion in budget cuts; police estimated around 30,000, and hundreds of youths clashed with police near the central station. In France, student blockades over overcrowded schools that began in late September spread nationwide, disrupting more than 700 secondary schools, with about 5,000 arrests.

The bond market saw it months ago. Streets are the last place a debt problem shows up, not the first.

The exit door: Europe has used it before

When debt stress turns into a run on banks, governments close the exit. The eurozone has done it twice. In Cyprus in 2013, banks closed for almost two weeks, large uninsured deposits at the biggest banks were partly converted into shares, and controls on withdrawals and transfers lasted until April 2015. In Greece in 2015, banks shut for three weeks, cash withdrawals were capped at €60 a day, and the last controls were lifted only in September 2019, four years later.

Both followed the same cascade:

  1. Bond stress. Yields jump and spreads widen.

  2. Bank stress. Banks hold their own government’s bonds, so their balance sheets weaken.

  3. Deposit flight. Savers move money abroad or into cash.

  4. Capital controls. The exit is closed to stop the run.

  5. Losses. Haircuts land on deposits or bonds.

The honest counterweight: Cyprus and Greece were small and isolated, and the ECB now has tools it lacked in 2013, such as its Transmission Protection Instrument to cap spreads. A cascade in France or Belgium is far less likely. But if it ever happened in a core country, it would also be far harder to contain.

Ratings come last; foreign capital votes first

Rating agencies confirm what markets have already priced. Mortgage securities were rated AAA into 2008; Greece was cut to junk in 2010 after its yields had already exploded. The first vote comes from foreign capital, and it looks like a quiet buyers’ strike long before it looks like a crisis: weaker auctions, a shrinking foreign share of the debt, wider spreads.

That vote is not settled. Japanese money is leaving the US faster than Europe, and has even come back to French bonds lately. Europe’s bill is looming, not due. Which is exactly why it pays to watch the flows rather than wait for the downgrade.

What it means for savers and gold

For European savers the lesson of Euroclear, Cyprus and Greece is the same: where your money sits matters as much as how much you have. Deposits sit inside the banking system, which is where capital controls bite first. Physical gold held outside it was the asset that could not be frozen at the ATM in 2013 or 2015. We looked at the history of confiscation, and its limits, in Could Europe Take Your Gold?

The counterweight remains real yields. With US 10-year yields above 5%, holding a metal that pays nothing has a real cost. The more the bill for Europe’s debt becomes visible in its spreads, the more that cost looks worth paying.

What to watch

  1. French and Belgian spreads over the Bund. France’s 149 bp peak of 2 October, and 100 bp for Belgium.

  2. Auction demand. Falling bid-to-cover ratios at French and Belgian debt auctions are the first sign of a buyers’ strike.

  3. Japan’s monthly flow data. Whether Japanese selling shifts from US to European bonds.

  4. Euroclear. Any EU move to use the frozen assets, and any Russian attempt to enforce the Moscow ruling abroad.

  5. The ECB. Whether it ever has to activate its Transmission Protection Instrument.
