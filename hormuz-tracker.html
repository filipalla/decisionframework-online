Visible tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz has been close to zero since the closure began on 28 February 2026: under one tanker a day against 47 before the war. Yet oil keeps reaching the market, around the strait by pipeline and through it with transponders switched off. The gap between the ship count and the barrels is the story.

Why the ship count and the barrels disagree

Public ship-tracking reads AIS, the transponder every large ship should broadcast. Dark tankers switch it off, so they vanish from the count while still carrying oil. Pipelines that bypass the strait carry more. That is why estimates of how much oil still leaves the Gulf range widely, depending on who counts and how.

The crude shortage and the fuel shortage

By the autumn, crude flows had largely recovered by other routes. The fuel shortage has not: diesel margins stayed near crisis levels. Oil you can load today against oil on a screen tells the same story: when physical Brent trades well above the futures, supply is still tight, whatever the export estimates say.

Watch the diesel, not only the strait. A reopening would show first in freight rates and physical premiums, and only weeks later in the fuel prices that drive inflation.

Why it matters beyond oil

Energy is the main reason inflation stayed sticky in 2026, and inflation decides what central banks do with rates. The strait therefore feeds straight into the bigger question behind the Desk: are we protecting money against inflation or against deflation?
