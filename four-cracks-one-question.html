Four Cracks, One Question

Nvidia under a cloud, a week of system failures, a gold short that never covers and PIMCO’s call to lock in yields. Four separate stories, until you put them side by side.

This week, four stories are playing out in four different worlds: Silicon Valley, the systems that run daily life, the gold futures pits in New York and the bond market. On the surface they have nothing to do with each other. So take them one at a time first.

Theme 1 · Nvidia: the revenue story under a cloud

Tonight, after the US close (results around 22:00, the call at 23:00 Brussels time), the most important stock in the market reports its quarter. Consensus sits near $108.8 billion in revenue and $2.47 in earnings per share.

Nvidia says its China compute business went to zero after the April 2025 export ban. In May, the short seller Culper Research published 40 pages arguing otherwise. It traced billions of dollars of Nvidia-powered servers through Megaspeed, a Singapore buyer, and its Malaysian subsidiary Speedmatrix, with filings that in Culper’s reading point to Alibaba money behind the purchases. Its estimate: more than 20% of Nvidia’s compute revenue was still driven by China, through the back door.

The market shrugged: the stock rose about 3% that day. Then the story kept growing. Megaspeed is under investigation in Singapore and has hired a US law firm to count its GPUs. Nvidia’s board has opened an independent investigation into whether its CEO was targeted through his relationship with a Megaspeed executive. The stock is down roughly a quarter since May.

Culper’s sharpest point is still ahead of us. Beijing is now shutting Nvidia out in favour of domestic chips, so the hidden China revenue may now really be going to zero. Analysts treat China as upside. Culper calls it an air gap in this year’s numbers.

Update · the commitments off the balance sheet

A tally of the latest 10-Q and 10-K filings (via ZeroHedge, using Morgan Stanley’s framework) puts the off-balance-sheet commitments of the hyperscalers, Nvidia and Broadcom at $3.56 trillion, up from Morgan Stanley’s $3.29 trillion count in August. Nvidia’s own figure is about $530 billion, against $348 billion in August, including roughly $108 billion of guarantees and backstops for others, plus about $125 billion more in reported potential residual-value support. When the chipmaker starts guaranteeing the debt and the resale value behind its own customers’ purchases, the line between revenue and financing gets thin. That is the same AI debt the banks are now trying to sell.

The question for tonight

Does the guidance show the gap? Watch how Nvidia talks about China and Southeast Asia, whether the call gets questions on Megaspeed and the board investigation, and whether the guarantees and purchase commitments grew again.

Theme 2 · The week the systems failed

In five days, four things broke.

  • Thursday 12 November: Microsoft’s Outlook, Teams, SharePoint and Copilot go down for more than a day, for users in over 70 countries.

  • Friday 13 November: US air traffic control loses communications and radar feeds. Every airport in the country stops departures for more than four hours, the longest nationwide flight suspension since 9/11. Recovery took days.

  • Monday 16 November: a “software bug in on-premise hardware” at JPMorgan Chase delays transactions with other banks. Customers at Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, US Bank, Truist and Capital One report trouble seeing balances and moving money.

  • Monday afternoon: United Airlines reports a systemwide technology outage and delays departures again.

For these four, officials say there is no evidence of an attack, and each operator names a different technical cause. About 60 air traffic controllers have reportedly challenged the government’s account of the ground stop.

Connected or not, the lesson is the same. Daily life now runs through a handful of systems, and when one of them fails, everyone who depends on it stops too.

Update · 17 November · then came the real attacks

The four failures above were, by every account, technical. The confirmed cyber incidents below are a different matter, and as far as anyone knows they are unrelated to those failures and to each other.

Optimizely. On 16 November, malicious JavaScript was served to visitors of 178 organisations through one shared tool, Optimizely’s web-testing snippet, including Zoom, the BBC, CNN, Salesforce, IKEA, HSBC, Equifax, Air France-KLM and Ledger. Security researcher Scott Helme of Report URI found it in 188 of the 1,611 snippets his team checked; the code skimmed payment cards, swapped crypto wallet addresses and targeted iPhones. Optimizely confirmed the incident early on 17 November.

Fugaku. RIKEN, the government-backed institute that runs Japan’s flagship supercomputer, confirmed unauthorised access to the systems used to operate it. The attackers got in through the public status page, the machine was taken offline, and data on about 4,600 registered users and stored research may have leaked.

And Europe’s fridges. Two weeks earlier, overnight into 2 November, attackers remotely changed the settings on refrigeration controls in supermarkets across Europe, warming fridges and freezers. Delhaize reported an “IT incident” in several dozen stores, mostly in Belgium; Carrefour, Ahold Delhaize and others were hit in Sweden, the Netherlands, Ireland, Luxembourg and Estonia, after an earlier wave on 11 October. Food was thrown out by the tonne. One refrigeration supplier blamed “a component supplied by a third party”. There is still no confirmed culprit.

The lesson is the same as the outages, sharper: you don’t hack 178 websites, or a thousand supermarket freezers, you hack the one component they all share.

The open question

If these are four unrelated accidents in one week, what does that say about how close to its limits the system is running?

Theme 3 · The gold short nobody covers

In COMEX gold futures, the swap dealer category, which includes the bullion banks, has held a net short position all year that barely moves.

The position is down only about 11% since January. With total open interest near 405,000 contracts, this one group is net short about half of all COMEX gold futures.

To be fair, much of that is hedging and market-making against positions elsewhere: swap dealers usually hold the matching short against the hedge funds’ longs. It is not a straight bet against gold. But the concentration is real, and it is stubborn. A handful of institutions sit on one side of the paper gold market, and their stance has hardly changed since January.

The open question

What happens to roughly 640 tonnes of paper short if it is ever forced to cover? The next Commitments of Traders report comes out on Friday.

Theme 4 · PIMCO: “yields like this come once a generation”

On 16 November, PIMCO’s Group CIO Dan Ivascyn and strategist Esteban Burbano made an unusually blunt call: lock in bond yields now, for the next three to five years.

Their case: yields are near their highest in about 25 years. That is driven by the Middle East energy shock and a sharp rise in real interest rates, while inflation expectations have risen much less. A broad US bond index yields in the mid-5% range against core inflation in the mid-2% range, a cushion of roughly 3% that did not exist before the 2022 bond rout.

PIMCO has moved to longer maturities and up in quality: government bonds, senior asset-backed securities and agency mortgages. It avoids high yield and bank loans, sees credit spreads near historically tight levels and keeps cash in reserve in case markets are disrupted. On stocks it does not hedge: very expensive relative to earnings and real rates, a level that has tended to come before weak returns.

Not everyone agrees. Morgan Stanley went maximum overweight global equities this week, with an S&P 500 target of 8,700 for the end of 2027, and thinks markets are too hawkish on rates. Goldman expects two more Fed hikes. The big institutions are split, and that alone tells you something.

Morgan Stanley on gold

Its 2027 outlook names gold its top commodity pick, at $5,250 an ounce: about 36% above spot near $3,865, even while it expects a firmer dollar (DXY 105) and a 5% 10-year Treasury.

Goldman on gold

Cut its end-2027 target to $4,650 this month, still about 20% above spot. Two of the biggest banks disagree on how far, not on the direction.

That is the striking part. The most bullish bank on stocks this week is also the most bullish on gold. It expects to win with both: AI spending carries the stock market, while gold is the hedge against everything that spending, and the debt behind it, could break.

For gold investors, this is the strongest counter-argument of the week. Gold pays no coupon. When a high-quality bond pays you 3% above inflation, holding a metal that pays nothing has a real cost. High real yields have traditionally been gold’s toughest opponent. You can follow that cost every day on the real rates page.

But read PIMCO’s own list of risks to its view: a prolonged energy shock; strain on consumer lending and commercial real estate from higher real rates; AI overinvestment; and US fiscal sustainability. Its base case also assumes the Middle East settles down within several months.

The open question

The bond only pays off if inflation stays tame and the issuer stays sound. PIMCO itself names a long energy shock and US fiscal sustainability as the threats. How confident are you in both for five years?

Where the four meet

Now put them side by side.

Everything on the list is a promise. Nvidia promises its revenue is what it says it is. The banks promise your balance is there when the app says so. The bullion banks’ paper short promises metal that, by definition, is not sitting behind every contract. And PIMCO’s bond is a promise too: a government or company promising to pay you, in dollars, for five years.

Notice that PIMCO’s own risk list reads like this week’s themes. “AI overinvestment” is Theme 1. Stress in lending and real estate from higher rates is the kind of strain that shows up first in the plumbing of Theme 2. “US fiscal sustainability” is the promise behind every Treasury. Even the strongest case for bonds this week rests on the same things that make gold worth owning.

Update · 17 November · the AI debt is already moving

PIMCO’s “AI overinvestment” risk is showing up on bank balance sheets. HSBC has reportedly sold its $300 million piece of the loan behind Oracle’s Stargate campus in New Mexico, Project Jupiter, at roughly 85 cents on the dollar, a loss of about $45 million. In September the same $18 billion loan was quoted at 89–91 cents, after Oracle invoked force majeure over stuck permits and a delayed gas pipeline, with Oracle’s credit rating one notch above junk. Ten days ago, ING put a dozen data center loans worth about $500 million up for auction, including a $25 million slice of that same New Mexico campus, with bids due on Thursday 19 November. 91 cents, then 85: Thursday shows where the next mark lands. When the banks that financed the build-out start selling the paper, the price they get tells you what the AI promise is worth.

And notice who else hedges. Morgan Stanley, maximum overweight on stocks and calling for a stronger dollar, still names gold its top commodity for 2027. Even the bulls want the one asset that is not a promise.

Concentration. One chipmaker carries the stock market. A few platforms carry daily life. A few banks carry the short side of gold. And the bond market leans on a US government whose fiscal sustainability PIMCO itself lists as a key risk. Efficient in good times, fragile when tested.

Trust in systems you cannot see into. You cannot audit Nvidia’s end customers, look inside JPMorgan’s servers, see who would deliver the metal behind 640 tonnes of paper shorts, or know what inflation will be in 2031.

A bond pays you for trusting the system. Gold pays you nothing, because it asks for no trust at all.

PIMCO is right that bonds pay you to wait and gold does not. That is the honest trade-off. But it is not either/or. The bond is income. Gold is insurance: no revenue to guide, no server to fail, no counterparty and no coupon that depends on someone else’s promise.

On Monday, millions of people opened their banking app and read “Some services aren’t available.” That is what owning only claims on things feels like. In a week like this, ask yourself how much of your wealth is a claim on someone else’s promise, and how much is the thing itself.
